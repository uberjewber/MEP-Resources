Equations

Saturation pressure is Hyland–Wexler; humidity ratio from wet bulb uses ASHRAE eq. 33, or eq. 35 below freezing. Enthalpy is referenced to 0 °F dry air in IP and 0 °C in SI, so the two enthalpy numbers are not simple conversions of each other. Pressure comes from the standard atmosphere at the site elevation.

Reading the chart

Lines through the state point trace each property to its scale: wet bulb and dew point at the saturation curve, enthalpy on the scale beyond it, dry bulb along the bottom and humidity ratio on the right. Wet-bulb and enthalpy lines run nearly parallel, which is why that pair can't be used as inputs.

How the mix is computed

Each stream's humidity ratio comes from dry bulb and thermodynamic wet bulb. Dry-air mass flow is volume divided by specific volume. Humidity ratio and enthalpy are mass-weighted, and the mixed dry bulb is solved back from h = 0.240·t + W(1061 + 0.444·t).

Why not average by CFM

A CFM-weighted dry bulb ignores that warm, humid air is less dense. For typical outside/return mixes the error is a few tenths of a degree, shown in the result panel. It grows with large temperature spreads, high elevation or one very humid stream.

Cooling coil

Condensate is dry-air mass flow × (W entering − W leaving), at 8.34 lb/gal. Total load is ṁ[(h₁ − h₂) − (W₁ − W₂)·h_w] with condensate leaving at the leaving dry bulb. Sensible load is cooling at constant humidity ratio; latent is the rest. The apparatus dew point is where the coil line, extended, meets saturation, and bypass factor is (t₂ − ADP)/(t₁ − ADP).

Fogging

If the mixing line crosses saturation, the calculator reports the equilibrium state: saturated air at a slightly higher temperature, with the excess moisture carried as liquid.

How the inputs work

Enter any two of airflow, size, velocity and friction rate. Typing in a calculated field makes it an input and releases the oldest one; click a tag to release or keep a value. Velocity plus friction plus airflow (or size) treats velocity and friction as maximums, and the stricter one governs.

Friction

Darcy–Weisbach, Δp = f·(L/D)·ρV²/2, with the Colebrook friction factor and the selected roughness. Galvanized steel at standard air is the basis of the ASHRAE friction chart and slide-rule duct calculators. Fitting losses are not included.

Rectangular duct

Height is held fixed and width is solved. Friction uses the Huebscher equivalent, De = 1.30·(ab)^0.625 / (a+b)^0.25; velocity uses the actual area. Aspect ratios above 4:1 are flagged.

Actual air

Density from dry-air ideal gas at the air temperature and the site pressure for the elevation above; viscosity from Sutherland's law. Airflow is then actual volume.

Sensible air capacity

1.08 = 0.075 lb/ft³ × 0.24 Btu/lb·°F × 60 min/h, standard air at sea level.

Water capacity

500 = 8.33 lb/gal × 60 min/h × 1.0 Btu/lb·°F. Glycol solutions use a lower factor; enter it from the fluid's density and specific heat.

Air changes per hour

Airflow is the supply (or exhaust) rate through the room; volume is the room's net air volume.

How the inputs work

Every field but one is an input. The calculated field is dashed. Typing into it makes it an input, and the field you edited least recently becomes the calculated one. Click a field's Input tag to calculate that field instead.

Sensible factor

1.08 assumes standard air. "Site elevation" scales it by barometric pressure at the elevation set above, holding 70 °F; at 5,000 ft it drops to about 0.90. For actual CFM at other temperatures, scale by 530 / (t + 460).

SI units

The same relations are used with converted units: 1.08 becomes about 1.207 W per L/s·K, 500 becomes about 4,181 W per L/s·K, and the air change constant becomes 3.6 (L/s to m³/h).
